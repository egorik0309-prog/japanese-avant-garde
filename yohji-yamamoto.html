Yohji
Yamamoto

Challenging the boundaries of fashion through oversized silhouettes, deconstruction and a distinctive vision of beauty.

TOKYO / PARIS

A DESIGNER'S POINT OF VIEW

Yohji Yamamoto's clothing is designed to move, not simply to decorate.

Loose proportions, layered construction and a considered use of black give his work a distinctive presence. The silhouette often feels open and fluid, leaving space between the body and the garment.

Rather than offering a single idea of beauty, Yamamoto's work invites the wearer to find their own way of inhabiting the clothes.

This balance of structure and freedom is part of what makes his work recognisable across decades.

Early Life

Yohji Yamamoto was born in Japan in 1943. He initially studied law at Keio University in Tokyo, but later decided to follow his interest in fashion.

In 1969, he graduated from Bunka Fashion College. In 1972, he introduced his own fashion label, Y's. His early experiences helped him develop a different approach to clothing and design.

Key Collections

Yamamoto presented his first collection in Paris in 1981. His unconventional designs challenged the dominant fashion trends of the time through loose shapes, unusual proportions and dark colours.

In 1984, he introduced his first menswear collection in Paris. Another important milestone came in 2002, when he joined forces with adidas to launch Y-3, combining avant-garde fashion with sportswear.

Y-3 became an important example of how high fashion and sportswear could influence each other.

Philosophy

Yamamoto's philosophy is based on freedom, individuality and the rejection of traditional fashion rules.

He is known for oversized silhouettes, asymmetrical cuts, layered fabrics and unfinished details. Black is one of the most recognizable elements of his work.

Rather than focusing on perfect shapes, Yamamoto explores movement, personality and the relationship between the human body and clothing.

Influence

Yohji Yamamoto helped transform the international fashion industry by introducing a new way of thinking about clothing and personal style.

His work influenced generations of designers who experiment with shape, proportion, gender and construction. His designs also helped establish Japanese avant-garde fashion as an important movement on the international stage.
